# Service recovery

How to answer a customer when something has gone wrong: a bug, a late delivery, a wrong charge, an outage or a mistake by the support team. The support skills apply this file to every reply and macro that handles a failure, and the QA review scores against it.

## The three kinds of fairness

Research on complaint handling explains a customer's response to a recovery by how fair they judge three things:

- **The outcome** (distributive justice): what they get. The refund, credit, replacement or fix.
- **The process** (procedural justice): how they get it. Speed, how much effort it took, how clearly the steps and timing were explained.
- **The treatment** (interactional justice): how they were dealt with. Honesty, a real apology, respect, an explanation of what happened.

A meta-analysis of complaint handling found that the outcome mostly drives satisfaction with the single complaint, while cumulative satisfaction, which drives loyalty, depends slightly more on the treatment than on the outcome (Gelbrich and Roschk, 2011, *Journal of Service Research*). A generous refund given curtly does less for the relationship than a fair one given well.

## What the research warns against

- **Counting on the recovery paradox.** A well-handled failure can leave a customer more satisfied than if nothing had gone wrong, but a meta-analysis found that lift does not carry over to repurchase intentions or word of mouth (de Matos, Henrique and Rossi, 2007, *Journal of Service Research*). Never plan on a failure making a customer more loyal.
- **Silence about time.** Telling the customer how long the recovery will take and what the steps are strengthens their positive feelings about it (Valentini, Orsingher and Polyakova, 2020, *Marketing Letters*). Every reply to a failure says when the next update or the fix comes.
- **Empty gestures.** An apology or explanation that is not followed by what was promised is a second failure (a "double deviation"), and customers judge it worse than no recovery attempt at all. Promise only what the policy allows and the team will do.

## Failure reply checklist

Every failure reply and macro passes all of these:

1. It acknowledges the specific problem in the customer's terms, not a generic "issue".
2. It apologises once, specifically, without blaming the customer, a colleague or a supplier.
3. It explains what happened as far as is known, and says plainly what is not yet known.
4. It says what will be done and by when, or when the next update comes.
5. It offers only what the `## Policies` section of `04-macros.md` allows without approval; anything else is "I've asked <team> and will reply by <time>".
6. It promises nothing with no policy line behind it: no dates for fixes, no compensation, no legal positions.
7. It asks the customer for as little as possible, and for nothing the team already has.
8. It leaves no internal notes, ticket links or other customers' details in the text.
